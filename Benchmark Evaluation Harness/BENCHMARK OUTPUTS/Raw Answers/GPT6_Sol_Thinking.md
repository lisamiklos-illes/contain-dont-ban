# Question 1 — Replication-associated features

What sequence-level features of DNA or RNA are associated with the ability of a nucleic-acid molecule to participate in self-replication? Explain the biological relevance of these features.

Sequence features associated with replication fall into three broad categories: sites that recruit copying machinery, sequences that create a suitable template or functional structure, and sequences that encode or provide catalytic activity. However, there is no universal “self-replication sequence”: a molecule’s replication competence depends on the particular enzymes, host, and chemical conditions available. Being copied as a template is also different from catalyzing one’s own copying.[^1_1][^1_2][^1_3]

## Principal replication-associated features

| Sequence-level feature | Biological relevance | Important qualification |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Complementary base-pairing potential | A DNA or RNA strand specifies a complementary strand through base pairing. Copying that complement can regenerate the original sequence, allowing hereditary information to propagate. | This is a general property of nucleic acids—not a diagnostic motif distinguishing replicating from nonreplicating molecules. [^1_1][^1_2] |
| Origins of replication and initiator-recognition sites in DNA | Origin-associated sequences recruit or organize replication-initiation machinery and support local strand opening. These features help determine where copying starts. | Their significance is specific to the replication system; the presence of an origin does not make DNA independent of replication proteins. [^1_1][^1_4] |
| Locally easy-to-unwind sequences | AT-rich regions occur at many DNA origins and facilitate the initial separation of template strands. More generally, sequence-dependent duplex stability affects template accessibility. | AT richness alone does not establish a functional origin or demonstrate replication competence. [^1_4] |
| Primer-binding or initiation-compatible sequences | Complementarity to a primer positions an extendable 3′ end for polymerization. In some systems, sequence-dependent folding can also position an end for initiation. | DNA polymerases require a primer, but a dedicated, pre-existing primer-binding motif is not universal: cellular primases synthesize primers during replication. [^1_1][^1_5] |
| RNA replication signals, often at or near the termini | Conserved terminal sequences and other cis-acting elements—features on the RNA being copied—can recruit an RNA-dependent RNA polymerase and direct initiation. | The relevant elements can extend into coding regions; they are not necessarily confined to untranslated regions. [^1_6][^1_7][^1_8] |
| Inverted repeats, complementary regions, and structure-forming sequences | These can generate hairpins, stem–loops, pseudoknots, or long-range interactions that organize replication complexes or expose initiation sites. For example, interactions between distant regions of some RNA genomes bring their ends together during initiation. | Structure and position matter, not merely the presence of repeats. A hairpin may promote replication in one context and obstruct copying in another. [^1_9][^1_10][^1_7] |
| Coding sequences for replication proteins, with appropriate expression signals | A molecule may encode proteins needed for its propagation, such as a polymerase or replication initiator. This links the genetic information being copied to production of the copying machinery. | A coding region contributes only if it is expressed and functional. Conversely, a template can be replicated when the necessary proteins are supplied by another source. [^1_11][^1_7] |
| Catalytic RNA sequence and fold | Some RNA sequences fold into ribozymes that catalyze RNA synthesis or ligation. Such molecules can contribute directly to replication chemistry rather than only encode protein catalysts. | Generic ribozyme activity is not equivalent to self-replication; replication requires production of the relevant sequence, not simply cleavage or ligation of an unrelated substrate. [^1_2][^1_3] |
| End-maintenance sequences in linear genomes | Telomeric repeats provide specialized chromosome-end substrates that help address the difficulty of fully copying and maintaining linear DNA ends. | These are features of particular linear-genome systems, not universal requirements for replication. [^1_1][^1_4] |

## Sequence can affect replication in opposing ways

A sequence must often balance functional folding against template accessibility. Stable RNA structures can create polymerase-recognition sites or catalytic active sites, yet those same structures can make the RNA harder to copy. Likewise, stable pairing helps preserve sequence information but makes separation of template and product more difficult. Consequently, neither high GC content nor extensive secondary structure is, by itself, evidence of replication ability.[^1_2][^1_10]

For a catalytic RNA replicator, this tension is especially important: the molecule needs a folded state to function as a catalyst and an accessible state to function as a template. Successful propagation also requires sufficient copying accuracy to preserve the sequence features responsible for that function.[^1_3][^1_10]

## What these features do—and do not—establish

It is useful to distinguish three levels:

1. Template competence: the molecule can be copied by available machinery.
2. Replicon competence: it contains the necessary cis-acting information to be propagated in a specified biological system, while relying on enzymes supplied by that system.
3. Catalytic self-replication: the nucleic acid itself supplies catalytic activity that contributes to producing copies of its own sequence. For complementary-strand copying, this requires both the original-to-complement and complement-to-original steps.[^1_11][^1_1][^1_3]

Thus, the strongest sequence-level evidence is a coherent combination of initiation signals, appropriately positioned structural elements, and any required coding or catalytic functions—not an isolated motif, repeat, or compositional bias. Even that combination establishes a hypothesis about replication competence, rather than proof of self-sustained replication.[^1_9][^1_10][^1_3]

# Question 2 — Requirements for self-replication

What distinguishes a nucleic-acid molecule that can self-replicate from one that can only be copied using externally supplied biological machinery?

The key distinction is between **being a template** and **regenerating the capacity to replicate**. A molecule that is merely copyable supplies sequence information while externally supplied machinery performs the copying. A self-replicating molecule—or a defined, mutually replicating molecular system—produces functional descendants that can participate in the same replication cycle again. The distinction therefore concerns a complete, repeatable process, not simply the presence of a replication-associated sequence.[^2_1][^2_2]

## What self-replication requires

Beyond being recognizable as a template, a self-replicating system needs:

- A mechanism for making new copies. This can involve an RNA catalyst that assembles additional copies of itself, or mutually catalytic RNAs that synthesize each other. Base pairing alone does not supply all the chemistry needed to form a new nucleic-acid backbone.[^2_2][^2_3]
- A complete reproduction cycle. In complementary-strand replication, producing the complement is only an intermediate step: the system must also regenerate the original sequence. Alternatively, a ligation-based replicator can assemble copies from supplied fragments without a nucleotide-by-nucleotide complementary-strand cycle.[^2_1][^2_2]
- Functional, reusable products. Newly synthesized molecules must become available to act as templates or catalysts rather than remain trapped in inactive complexes. They must retain the sequence and structure needed to support subsequent rounds.[^2_3][^2_2]
- Sufficient productivity and fidelity for continued propagation. Making a partial copy, a nonfunctional copy, or a single product that cannot support another round does not demonstrate sustained self-replication. Sustained amplification is stronger evidence than a one-time synthesis event.[^2_3][^2_1]


## “Self-replicating” depends on the system boundary

The term is used at different levels of autonomy, which should not be conflated.


| Usage | What the nucleic acid contributes | What remains external |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Externally assisted copying | A template and any required recognition or primer-binding sites | The copying catalyst; for example, a separately supplied polymerase |
| Biological self-amplification | A template plus genes encoding a replication enzyme | A host’s translation machinery, energy, building blocks, and other required cellular functions |
| Molecular self- or cross-replication | Catalytic nucleic acid that helps generate additional functional copies of itself or its replication partner | Substrates, chemical energy, suitable conditions, and potentially externally imposed physical cycles |

These distinctions explain why RNA amplification by a supplied polymerase ribozyme does not, by itself, demonstrate that the RNA catalyst can reproduce itself. Likewise, an RNA encoding its own polymerase can be called self-amplifying within a cell while still depending on host translation machinery.[^2_4][^2_1]

## Self-replication does not mean independence from all inputs

Even a molecular self-replicator needs matter, energy, and suitable conditions. The important question is which inputs are supplied: nucleotides or prepared RNA fragments are substrates, whereas an externally supplied polymerase supplies replication machinery. The complexity of those supplied substrates also affects how autonomous the system really is.[^2_1][^2_3]

For example, **Lincoln and Joyce** demonstrated a pair of RNA enzymes that catalyzed each other’s assembly from four supplied RNA fragments, supporting sustained exponential amplification without protein enzymes. This established self-sustained cross-replication under the experimental conditions—not autonomous synthesis of the entire system from simple monomers.[^2_3]

Thus, an origin, hairpin, primer-binding site, or polymerase-binding motif can make a molecule a suitable replication substrate. What distinguishes self-replication is closure of the reproductive cycle: the products replenish the functional templates and catalytic capacity required for further reproduction, within an explicitly defined environment.[^2_2][^2_1]

# Question 3 — Replication potential

Given a DNA or RNA sequence, what information would you need to determine whether it has self-replication potential? Explain what can and cannot be inferred from the sequence alone.

A DNA or RNA sequence can support a prediction of replication potential, but it generally cannot establish self-replication on its own. The question must be defined relative to a system: replication in which host or chemical environment, with which externally supplied components, and at what level of autonomy? Both DNA-origin activity and RNA-catalyzed replication depend on factors beyond the base sequence.[^3_1][^3_2]

## Information needed

| Information | Why it matters |
| :-- | :-- |
| The intended meaning of “self-replication” | Being copied by supplied enzymes, propagating as a replicon in a host, and catalyzing one’s own reproduction are different capabilities. Evidence for one does not establish the others. [^3_3][^3_2] |
| The complete molecule and its physical form | You need to know whether the sequence is complete or a fragment, DNA or RNA, single- or double-stranded, and linear or circular. These properties determine how candidate initiation sites, ends, and structures should be interpreted; a base-letter sequence alone does not fully specify the physical substrate. [^3_4][^3_5] |
| The host or cell-free environment | The relevant polymerases, initiators, accessory factors, and—where proteins must be expressed—transcription and translation systems must be available and compatible. For chromosomal DNA, chromatin and cell state can determine whether a candidate origin is actually used. [^3_4][^3_1][^3_6] |
| Which components are supplied externally | Supplying nucleotides, supplying preformed RNA fragments, and supplying a polymerase represent different dependencies. This inventory is essential to deciding whether the molecule is merely being copied or whether the defined system regenerates its replication capacity. [^3_3][^3_2] |
| Functional sequence and structure annotations | Relevant evidence includes origins, terminal replication signals, primer-recognition regions, intact replication-related genes and expression signals, or a plausible catalytic RNA fold. Their arrangement and compatibility matter as much as their individual presence. [^3_4][^3_5][^3_2] |
| Chemical and kinetic conditions | Temperature, ions, substrate chemistry, folding, strand accessibility, copying rate, fidelity, and degradation determine whether a plausible mechanism can actually sustain propagation. [^3_2] |
| Functional evidence | The decisive evidence concerns whether complete, functional descendants are produced and can support subsequent rounds—not merely whether a polymerase binds or a short extension product appears. [^3_3][^3_2] |

## What sequence alone can reveal

Sequence analysis can identify or predict:

- Similarity to characterized replicons or catalytic RNAs, supporting a hypothesis about mechanism and likely dependencies.
- Candidate cis-acting signals, such as origins or RNA terminal elements.
- Potential coding capacity, including replication-related open reading frames and apparent disruptive mutations.
- Possible secondary structures and long-range interactions that could support recognition, catalysis, or initiation.[^3_2][^3_4][^3_5]

These findings have unequal evidential weight. An exact match to a well-characterized construct is stronger evidence than a short motif match, but its known behavior still applies to specified conditions. Conversely, failure to find a familiar motif does not prove inability to replicate: some replication systems lack a simple consensus sequence.[^3_6][^3_1]

## What sequence alone cannot establish

### Whether predicted features function in context

A candidate origin need not be active, an open reading frame need not yield an active enzyme, and a predicted RNA fold need not be the catalytically active structure under the conditions tested. In metazoans, origin selection depends substantially on chromatin and cellular context; even in budding yeast, an origin-consensus motif alone is insufficient to identify an active origin.[^3_2][^3_6]

### Whether the complete reproductive cycle closes

Detecting an RNA polymerase gene or catalytic motif does not establish that the system can produce full-length copies, release them, and use those descendants in another round. For complementary-strand replication, both copying directions must work as a connected cycle.[^3_2]

A useful example is the RNA polymerase ribozyme **QT45**: researchers demonstrated synthesis of its complementary strand and synthesis of its own sequence in separate reactions. Nevertheless, the study distinguished these achievements from self-sustained replication because synthesis remained slow and low-yielding. Even direct evidence for both component reactions did not establish a sustained cycle.[^3_2]

### Whether propagation outpaces loss

Sequence alone does not reliably establish whether copying is sufficiently fast and accurate to overcome degradation, dilution, or production of inactive descendants. Those are properties of the molecule operating in an environment, not of its sequence in isolation.[^3_2]

## How to frame the conclusion

An assessment should separate three claims:

1. Sequence-supported potential: relevant features suggest a plausible replication mechanism.
2. Demonstrated replication competence: the molecule is copied in a specified host or reaction system.
3. Demonstrated self-sustained replication: functional descendants support continued reproduction under explicitly stated external inputs.[^3_3][^3_1][^3_2]

The appropriate conclusion from sequence analysis is therefore usually conditional: “This sequence is consistent with replication by mechanism X, provided factors Y and conditions Z are available.” Establishing self-replication requires functional evidence that the reproductive cycle actually continues.[^3_1][^3_2]

# Question 4 — Autonomous versus dependent replication

Explain the difference between autonomous self-replication, replication dependent on external enzymes, and non-replicating nucleic-acid molecules.

The difference is whether a nucleic acid participates in a system that regenerates its own replication capacity, is copied by machinery supplied from elsewhere, or does not undergo replication in the specified environment. These categories require an explicit system boundary: “autonomous within a host cell” is not the same as “independent of externally supplied biological machinery.”[^4_1][^4_2][^4_3]

## Comparison

| Category | Defining property | Example and limitation |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Autonomous self-replication, in the strict molecular sense | The molecule—or a defined molecular network—provides the catalytic activity needed to generate functional descendants that can repeat the reproductive cycle, without an externally supplied replication enzyme. | Cross-replicating RNA enzymes can catalyze each other’s assembly from supplied RNA fragments. The pair is the self-replicating system; neither member is independent of its partner. Substrates and suitable conditions remain external inputs. [^4_1] |
| Replication dependent on external enzymes | The nucleic acid serves as a template, but an essential copying catalyst comes from the host, a helper system, or the experimental reaction rather than being regenerated by the nucleic-acid system under consideration. | An RNA bearing appropriate replication signals can be copied by a replicase supplied by a separate helper RNA. It is replication-competent in that system, but not independently self-replicating. [^4_2] |
| Non-replicating nucleic acid | The molecule does not generate additional copies of itself under the conditions being considered. It may still be translated, perform another biological function, or be degraded. | Conventional non-amplifying mRNA is translated into protein without amplifying its RNA template; self-amplifying RNA additionally encodes replication machinery. [^4_4] |

## Why “autonomous” can be misleading

In genetics, autonomous replication often means that a replicon can replicate as a distinct genetic unit within a compatible cell. It does not necessarily mean independence from cellular enzymes, metabolism, or translation. A plasmid described as autonomously replicating in a host therefore should not automatically be classified as a chemically self-sufficient molecular replicator.[^4_3][^4_5]

Similarly, **self-amplifying RNA** encodes its own replicase, but host ribosomes must first translate that replicase, and the reaction still relies on cellular resources. It has greater control over its replication machinery than an RNA receiving replicase entirely in trans, but it is not autonomous outside the cellular environment.[^4_2][^4_6]

Even strict molecular self-replication does not mean independence from matter and energy. For example, the cross-replicating RNA enzymes demonstrated by Lincoln and Joyce required prepared RNA substrates; continued amplification involved replenishing those substrates. Their achievement was regeneration of the catalytic replicators, not synthesis of every required input from simple raw materials.[^4_7]

## “Non-replicating” is a contextual description

A molecule that does not replicate in one environment is not necessarily intrinsically impossible to copy. An RNA that requires a helper replicase may be non-replicating when that helper is absent and replication-competent when it is present. The classification can change without changing the RNA’s sequence.[^4_2]

Also, molecular activity is not equivalent to replication:

- Translation produces protein, not additional copies of the RNA template.
- RNA amplification produces additional RNA molecules.
- Replication within a cell does not necessarily confer the ability to spread between cells: engineered RNA replicons can retain replication functions while lacking the structural genes needed to produce infectious particles.[^4_4][^4_6]

The decisive questions are therefore: What is being reproduced, where does the copying catalyst come from, and can the resulting descendants sustain further rounds under the stated conditions?[^4_1][^4_2]

# Question 5 — Evidence of self-replication

What experimental evidence would be necessary to establish that a DNA or RNA molecule is genuinely capable of self-replication? How would you distinguish genuine replication from simple copying or amplification?

To establish genuine self-replication, you would need to show that a defined DNA or RNA system produces new, functional descendants that support further rounds of reproduction—and identify where the essential replication activity comes from. An increase in nucleic-acid abundance alone establishes amplification, not autonomy or self-replication. Externally powered copying systems can also amplify nucleic acids exponentially.[^5_1][^5_2]

## Essential experimental evidence

### 1. Demonstrate newly synthesized, correctly identified products

The evidence should establish that the products are new molecules, rather than surviving input material, contaminants, or a change in the detection signal. Complementary measurements should establish:

- Incorporation of supplied substrates into newly formed nucleic acid.
- The expected full length, sequence, and covalent integrity of the product.
- The identity of the strand produced, where complementary-strand synthesis is involved.

A short extension product, complementary fragment, or increased fluorescence does not demonstrate reproduction of the complete molecule. Likewise, joining fragments can count as reproduction, but the claim must explicitly state that prepared fragments—not individual nucleotides—were the supplied building blocks.[^5_3][^5_4][^5_5]

### 2. Show that the descendants are functional

Newly produced molecules must themselves serve as effective templates, catalysts, or genetic instructions for another round, depending on the claimed mechanism. The crucial distinction is between the original molecule repeatedly making products and its descendants acquiring the ability to continue the reproductive process. Product identity alone is insufficient if the products are inactive or inaccessible in stable complexes.[^5_5][^5_1]

For complementary-strand replication, the evidence must connect both directions:

$$
\text{original strand} \rightarrow \text{complementary strand}
\rightarrow \text{new original-sequence strand}.
$$

Demonstrating these reactions separately establishes important component capabilities, but not necessarily a functioning, repeated cycle.[^5_5]

### 3. Demonstrate sustained propagation beyond the initial seed

**Serial transfer** provides strong evidence: a fraction of the reaction is transferred into fresh substrates, and propagation continues through enough transfers that the original seed cannot plausibly account for the continuing activity. The experiment must distinguish descendants from carryover of the initial catalyst.[^5_2][^5_6]

However, serial transfer is not sufficient by itself. If fresh polymerase is supplied at every transfer, continued amplification demonstrates an externally supported copying system—not independence from that polymerase. The composition of every replenished reaction therefore matters.[^5_1]

### 4. Establish what causes replication

Mechanistic controls should test the claimed source of replication activity:


| Control or comparison | What it helps establish |
| :-- | :-- |
| No initial replicator | Whether products arise from background assembly or contamination rather than seed-dependent propagation |
| A replication-defective or catalytically inactive variant | Whether the proposed functional sequence is necessary |
| Disruption and, where feasible, restoration of a required interaction | Whether a predicted recognition site or structural interaction has the proposed causal role |
| Omission of supplied enzymes, helper molecules, or partners | Which activities are external dependencies and which are regenerated within the system |
| Functional testing of newly synthesized products | Whether descendants—not just the original input—can support subsequent reproduction |

These controls should be interpreted together: loss of activity after a mutation may reflect defective folding or recognition rather than loss of catalysis specifically. The goal is to connect the proposed mechanism to continued propagation, not merely to correlate a sequence with increased yield.[^5_4][^5_3][^5_1]

## Distinguishing copying, amplification, and self-replication

These are not mutually exclusive terms: self-replication can produce amplification. They describe different aspects of the process.[^5_2][^5_1]


| Observation | What it establishes | What it does not establish |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| A supplied polymerase makes a complementary strand | Template copying | That the template generates or regenerates its copying machinery |
| Nucleic-acid abundance increases, even exponentially | Amplification | Autonomous self-replication |
| A catalyst synthesizes another molecule with its own sequence | Self-synthesis or a component of a replication cycle | That the product is functional or that repeated cycles occur |
| Functional descendants sustain reproduction through repeated propagation, with essential catalytic capacity supplied or regenerated within the defined system | Strong evidence of self-sustained replication at that system boundary | Independence from substrates, energy, host functions, or physical interventions not included within that boundary |

Exponential growth is therefore supportive but not diagnostic. Conversely, failure to observe exponential growth does not by itself exclude self-reproduction: substrate depletion, product inhibition, and other kinetic constraints can limit amplification.[^5_3][^5_1][^5_2]

## A benchmark—and the appropriate limit on the claim

**Lincoln and Joyce** demonstrated two RNA enzymes that catalyzed each other’s synthesis from four supplied RNA fragments. Both products accumulated, and amplification persisted through repeated dilution into fresh substrates. That combination—defined chemistry, functional cross-catalysis, and sustained propagation—supported a claim of self-sustained cross-replication. It did not establish that either RNA replicated independently or that the system could manufacture its own substrates from simpler precursors.[^5_2]

The strongest experimental conclusion is therefore specific: identify the reproducing unit, demonstrate functional descendants and repeated cycles, quantify all supplied inputs, and state exactly which dependencies remain. That separates evidence of a self-replicating system from evidence that a nucleic acid is simply an effective substrate for external machinery.[^5_1][^5_2]
